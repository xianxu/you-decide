# California statewide propositions — November 3, 2026

Neutral research on all 14 statewide propositions in [the supplied reference ballot](sources/ballot-bt-114.txt), checked against the [Secretary of State's current guide](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/). “LAO” means the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office. Campaign arguments are attributed claims, not independently established outcomes. A missing submitted argument does not establish unanimous support. These summaries contain no vote recommendations.

## Proposition 1

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes authorizes $11.25 billion in housing bonds: $10 billion for housing programs and $1.25 billion for veterans' home loans. No denies this particular borrowing authority. Programs include affordable rentals, homeownership assistance, farmworker and student housing, and infrastructure; this is not a universal housing entitlement. [Official summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/), [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates General Fund repayment of $500–600 million annually for approximately 25 years. Veterans' loan payments would repay their portion; that program historically has covered its bond obligations. Borrowing brings spending forward but adds interest expense. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including veterans' and housing organizations, argue the measure would expand affordable housing and homeownership for vulnerable households and essential workers, with oversight. No argument against was submitted in the official guide. Its absence should not erase the budget tradeoff: General Fund debt service competes with other uses of state revenue. The last point is an inference from the financing structure, not an attributed opposition campaign. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/), [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/analysis.htm).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** LAO's projected housing assistance depends partly on combining the bonds with other public money. Authorization alone does not establish when individual developments will open or their final costs. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 2

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes raises the rainy-day fund's required-deposit ceiling from 10% to 20% of General Fund taxes, increases saving in unusually strong capital-gains years, and extends additional debt payments through 2040. No retains existing rules. Yes also changes when reserve funds count against the constitutional spending limit: upon withdrawal rather than deposit. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO expects larger reserves and possibly greater debt payments, without assigning a fixed dollar gain. Outcomes depend on future revenue and budget conditions; reserve accumulation is a timing and allocation decision, not a new revenue source. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including firefighter and business representatives, argue larger reserves would cushion schools, health care, and public safety against downturns. Opponents David Tangipa, Steven Choi, and Carl DeMaio argue the spending-limit change makes taxpayer rebates less likely and permits more eventual spending. These are competing assessments of budget discipline. [Official arguments and rebuttals](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/arguments-rebuttals.htm).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** The tradeoff is stronger future fiscal buffers against fewer dollars available for immediate uses. The supplied ballot says no opponents submitted, but the current state guide contains a substantive opposition argument; this summary follows the fuller source. [Supplied ballot](sources/ballot-bt-114.txt), [official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/arguments-rebuttals.htm).

## Proposition 3

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes makes existing higher personal-income-tax rates on high earners permanent. No leaves their scheduled expiration in 2031 intact; it does not immediately repeal them. These rates originated in Proposition 30 and were extended by Proposition 55. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** Relative to expiration, LAO estimates preservation of $5–15 billion annually, varying substantially with stock-market-linked income. Roughly 40% would support schools and community colleges, with the remainder supporting other programs and reserves. This is a revenue range, not guaranteed annual funding at its upper bound. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents argue preserving current rates avoids a large tax reduction for the wealthiest taxpayers and protects education and health services. Official opponents argue that making a temporary tax permanent rewards poor spending decisions and entrenches California's tax burden. Teacher and school organizations support the measure; taxpayer and business organizations oppose it. [Official summary and arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** The measure preserves a revenue source rather than fixing an annual appropriation for every named service. Budget effects begin against the post-expiration baseline, and reliance on volatile high-income receipts remains a central uncertainty. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 4

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes removes the general prohibition on public financing of state and local candidate campaigns and sets rules for future programs. No retains the prohibition and existing charter-city exceptions. Adoption itself does not establish or fund a statewide public-financing system. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates a few hundred thousand dollars annually for Fair Political Practices Commission guidance. Additional program costs could be significant but depend on governments subsequently choosing to create programs and their design. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including the League of Women Voters, argue enabling public financing could reduce donor influence and broaden competition. Official opponents, including taxpayer organizations, object to public money financing political campaigns and argue the measure lacks sufficient aggregate limits. These claims concern both the enabling law and possible future programs. [Official summary and arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** Future programs must require demonstrated support and spending limits; earmarked education, transportation, and public-safety funds cannot finance campaigns. Details remain local or legislative choices, so neither the eventual cost nor the effect on candidate diversity can be determined from this measure alone. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 5

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes separates recall removal from replacement selection by eliminating the second question on a state recall ballot. No retains the current simultaneous replacement election. Legislative vacancies generally move to a later special election; other offices generally use appointment. A recalled governor is succeeded by the lieutenant governor, with a subsequent election depending on the recall's timing. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO finds an unknown net effect: separate elections could cost millions, while shorter ballots and appointment-based replacements could save millions per recall. The result depends on which offices face successful recalls and whether elections can be consolidated. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** League of Women Voters and Common Cause proponents argue the measure prevents a replacement from winning with a small plurality and reduces partisan exploitation. Opponents Brian Jones and Joe Patterson argue it removes voters' immediate replacement choice and can prolong legislative vacancies. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/arguments-rebuttals.htm).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** The governor's timing rule matters: an early-term recall before the specified nomination deadline triggers a later statewide election; a later recall leaves the lieutenant governor serving the balance. The supplied ballot's “none submitted” labels do not reflect the guide's developed pro/con arguments. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/analysis.htm), [supplied ballot](sources/ballot-bt-114.txt).

## Proposition 37

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes authorizes up to $25 billion in revenue bonds for loans covering up to 17% of qualifying newly constructed homes' purchase prices. Buyers provide at least 3%, meet residency and income conditions, and repay the assistance. No leaves this new program unauthorized. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO identifies no direct state or local cost because borrower payments would cover bonds and administration. This differs from General Fund-supported borrowing. Program size depends on investor demand and whether its loans are competitive. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents say the program addresses middle-income families' down-payment barrier without taxpayer subsidy. The official guide has no submitted opposition argument. KQED nevertheless identifies Assemblymember Carl DeMaio as an opponent and reports criticism of developer benefits and potential taxpayer exposure; that criticism should not be confused with LAO's direct-cost estimate. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/), [KQED guide](https://www.kqed.org/voterguide/california/proposition-37).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** A voluntary qualified-builder option combines higher labor standards with more flexible construction-defect rules. Consequently, the policy includes changes affecting buyers' remedies as well as financing. The extent of additional construction and homeownership remains uncertain, rather than guaranteed by the bond ceiling. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 38

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes authorizes $8.4 billion in general obligation bonds for immunology and immunotherapy research. Roughly half supports one qualifying UC-affiliated research institute selected by the state; half supports competitive grants. No rejects this additional borrowing. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates $500–600 million annually for about 20 years. Revenue from discoveries could offset some or all repayment costs, but the amount and timing are uncertain and could take decades. Research success therefore does not remove the state's bond obligation. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including disease-research advocates, argue stable funding could accelerate treatments and cite audits and a California patient discount. Opponent Robert Kaplan argues research should be funded through normal budgets to preserve flexibility rather than long-term debt. KQED's reporting scrutinizes the intended institutional beneficiary, a meaningful issue when evaluating the allocation structure. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/), [KQED reporting](https://www.kqed.org/news/12102150/prop-38-borrows-8-4-billion-to-fund-immunology-research).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** The law generally requires a 20% California treatment discount and revenue sharing. Whether research produces marketable treatments, the resulting public return, and effects on health-system costs remain unknown; “designed” to offset costs is not a demonstrated forecast. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 39

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes requires government identification for in-person voting and matching final four identification-number digits on mail-ballot envelopes. It adds citizenship-verification efforts, reporting, and recurring audits, with free voter-identification cards on request. No retains existing identification and election-administration rules. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates annual state and local costs from tens of millions to low hundreds of millions of dollars. Costs depend on implementation, including card design and demand; any administrative savings are not expected to exceed costs. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents argue identification requirements would strengthen confidence and election integrity. Opponents, including civil-liberties and voting-rights organizations, argue the measure creates unnecessary obstacles for eligible voters, privacy risks, and expense. Neither confidence claims nor predictions of disenfranchisement establish a quantified effect by themselves. [Official summary and arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** Citizenship is already required for state and federal elections. The Legislature must clarify acceptable identification and what constitutes sufficient verification effort. LAO notes eligible people lacking identification could be among those removed from registration; the summaries reviewed do not quantify this group or demonstrate how much unlawful voting the proposal would prevent. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 40

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes enacts a one-time 5% wealth tax on qualifying billionaires resident on January 1, 2026, with payment beginning in 2027. Real estate and retirement assets generally are excluded. Ninety percent of proceeds must support health services. No rejects this tax. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates temporary receipts of tens of billions over several years, potentially accompanied by ongoing income-tax losses below $1 billion annually. Asset prices, tax avoidance, and relocation make receipts difficult to predict. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** SEIU-UHW and Bernie Sanders support using concentrated wealth to sustain health services. Official opponents argue the tax could damage investment and recurring revenues and question its safeguards. Claims that the measure reaches ordinary households or fully replaces health-funding losses require more than campaign language. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** Asset valuation and collection create administrative challenges. Propositions 41 and 42 could conflict with its spending-limit exemption and tax base or retroactive residency rule. LAO describes possible court-dependent blockage, not automatic cancellation merely because another measure passes. Section 8 also contains Proposition 40's own competing-measure clause. See the interaction note below. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm), [legal text, section 8](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-text-proposed-laws.pdf).

## Proposition 41

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes requires pre-ballot audits of programs supported by proposed special-tax initiatives, followed by recurring audits of programs financed by new or increased special taxes. It also restricts spending-limit exemptions for new taxes. No retains existing rules. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO expects audit costs initially in the low millions annually, growing over time, plus additional guide-publication costs. Approved taxes generally fund their audits; unsuccessful proposals can leave General Fund costs. Net savings are unknown and depend on effective recommendations being implemented. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including accountant and taxpayer organizations, emphasize transparency and more efficient spending. SEIU-UHW and Bernie Sanders oppose it as a mechanism to obstruct Proposition 40's health funding. The audit provisions and tax limitation are both substantive parts of the proposal. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** Audits start after an initiative reaches 25% of its required signatures, before qualification is certain. The measure requires identifying possible 10% program-cost reductions, not automatically enacting them. Its conflict clause seeks to void an entire competing initiative under specified conditions if 41 receives more affirmative votes; enforceability and actual conflict remain legal questions. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/analysis.htm), [legal text, section 5](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop41-text-proposed-laws.pdf).

## Proposition 42

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes prohibits new state taxes on ownership of personal property, including financial assets, and limits retroactive taxation. No leaves those future tax options available. Existing taxes and real-property taxation receive explicit protections; this is not a repeal of all property or investment-income taxes. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/analysis.htm), [legal text, proposed Article VIII](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop42-text-proposed-laws.pdf).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO says future revenue could be lower than otherwise because tax options become narrower. It supplies no dollar estimate, since future legislation, initiatives, and taxpayer responses are unknown. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents argue the measure protects savings and predictable financial planning from new or retroactive taxes. SEIU-UHW and Bernie Sanders argue the savings framing obscures an effort to defeat the billionaire tax and its health funding. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** The legal text reaches taxes enacted from January 1, 2026 and same-election initiatives. It includes a limited exception for legislatively enacted emergency taxes retroactive no more than 365 days. Its competing-measure clause could affect Proposition 40, but scope and enforceability require legal interpretation. Passing 42 is not, by itself, proof that every provision of 40 disappears. [Legal text, sections 4–5](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop42-text-proposed-laws.pdf), [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/analysis.htm).

## Proposition 43

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes requires two-thirds approval for voter-proposed local special taxes, including increases and extensions, beginning January 1, 2027. No preserves the current majority-vote route recognized by courts for such voter initiatives. Government-proposed special taxes already generally require two-thirds. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO expects the possibility of lower future local revenues than under current rules, without a dollar estimate. The outcome depends on which measures are proposed and whether voters approve them. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including taxpayer and business organizations, say one consistent supermajority rule protects taxpayers and restores the intended treatment of earmarked taxes. Opponents, including firefighter, teacher, and nursing organizations, argue a blocking minority would prevent majorities from funding local services. [Official summary and arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** This concerns taxes dedicated to specified purposes, rather than every local fiscal measure. Its prospective date matters when comparing it with other measures on the November 2026 ballot: the summary should not be used to assume retroactive invalidation of a particular local result. Assessing any specific local measure also requires checking its legal form and governing rules. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/analysis.htm), [legal text appearing after Proposition 42](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop42-text-proposed-laws.pdf).

## Proposition 44

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes imposes a 90%-of-revenue program-services requirement on covered nonprofit federally qualified community health centers, enforced through financial penalties. No leaves this new minimum unenacted. Program services are broader than only direct patient visits; the Attorney General would define qualifying expenses in greater detail. [Supplied ballot](sources/ballot-bt-114.txt), [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates enforcement costs in the low tens of millions annually, funded by clinic fees. Additional public costs depend on clinic responses, service use, and potential closures; those effects are unquantified. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents argue the rule redirects spending from executive excess and administration to charitable services. Official opponents, including medical and nursing organizations, argue inflexible spending requirements could close clinics and reduce access. The claim that hundreds would close is an opposition prediction, not LAO's quantified finding. [Official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** Temporary waivers and possible repayment of penalties create important implementation details. How expenses are classified may strongly affect compliance. The supplied ballot lists no supporters submitted, while the state guide includes a pro argument; neither label alone settles the policy's merits. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/analysis.htm), [supplied ballot](sources/ballot-bt-114.txt), [official arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/).

## Proposition 45

**Subject and vote effect.** Yes offers qualifying housing, infrastructure, and other designated projects an alternative CEQA process with tighter administrative and court deadlines. No retains current procedures. Changes also limit project alternatives and public-comment windows and narrow judicial remedies; this is broader than simply accelerating paperwork. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/analysis.htm).

**Fiscal effect.** LAO estimates initial annual public costs in the high tens of millions, potentially over $100 million, partly fee-funded. Longer-term effects could be larger in either direction: faster construction may reduce costs and raise revenues, while environmental harm could increase public expenses. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/analysis.htm).

**Arguments.** Official proponents, including housing, water, and hospital organizations, argue faster approvals would expand essential facilities and reduce costs while maintaining protections. Environmental and public-health opponents argue reduced review and litigation protections would harm communities without assuring affordability. [Official summary and arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/).

**Implementation and uncertainty.** Eligibility and applicants' choice of process determine reach. LAO identifies narrower tribal consultation, limited consideration of alternatives, and restrictions on stopping entire projects. The net effect on construction timing, environmental outcomes, and household prices cannot be inferred from proponents' or opponents' slogans alone. [LAO analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/analysis.htm).

## Interactions among Propositions 40, 41, and 42

These are three separate votes. The texts contain competing-measure clauses, rather than a simple rule that passage of either restriction automatically cancels the wealth tax.

- **40, section 8:** identifies competing billionaire-net-worth-tax measures and says 40 prevails if it receives more affirmative votes; it speaks of invalidating conflicting provisions of another measure. [Proposition 40 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-text-proposed-laws.pdf).
- **41, section 5:** identifies conflicting initiatives imposing different special-tax audit rules or containing spending-limit-exempt state taxes. If 41 receives more affirmative votes, its clause seeks to invalidate all provisions of the competing initiative. [Proposition 41 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop41-text-proposed-laws.pdf).
- **42, section 5:** similarly targets initiatives taxing ownership/control of defined personal assets or imposing retroactive liability and seeks whole-measure invalidation if it receives more affirmative votes. [Proposition 42 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop42-text-proposed-laws.pdf).

These clauses state what the measures seek to do; they do not establish a final judicial outcome. LAO says 40 **could** be stopped if 41 or 42 receives more yes votes because courts **could** find a conflict. Thus approval thresholds, relative affirmative votes, legal conflict, and enforceability all matter. CalMatters likewise reports potential litigation. Nonconflicting provisions should not be assumed automatically lost, nor should the two restrictive measures be assumed to cancel each other. [LAO interaction explanation](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm), [CalMatters reporting](https://calmatters.org/health/2026/09/california-wealth-tax-prop-40-41-42/).
